Cost reports 101

Where a Connecticut nursing home's money comes from, and where it goes

Every Connecticut nursing home that takes Medicaid files a yearly cost report with the Department of Social Services (DSS). It is the most detailed public record of a home's money. This page explains, in plain language, what those reports show, what they don't, and how the numbers on this site fit together. You don't need to read the DSS guidebook.

1. The yearly cycle 2. Who pays 3. Where a day's money goes 4. Reported, reviewed, paid 5. How the Medicaid rate is built 6. Try it with one home 7. Words to know
1

The yearly cycle, in four steps

A cost report covers one cost year, which in Connecticut runs October 1 to September 30. The money moves in a loop: what homes spend this year shapes the Medicaid rate they are paid in later years.

  1. Care and spendingStaff, food, supplies, building, managementOctober 1 to September 30
  2. Home files its reportEvery cost, line by line, signed by the owner or administrator"Reported" figures
  3. DSS reviewsRemoves or moves costs Medicaid does not cover; audits can follow"Reviewed" figures
  4. Rate is setOne Medicaid daily rate per home, from July 1"What Medicaid pays"

The loop repeats: DSS rebases rates on newer reports every two to four years, so today's spending shapes future rates.

The one thing to remember: cost report figures are the home's own filing. They come before DSS's review and before any audit. This site always labels them "reported".
2

Who pays for a resident's day

Most nursing home days in Connecticut are paid by Medicaid (HUSKY C), which covers long stays for people with limited income and assets. Medicare pays for short rehab stays after a hospital visit. The rest is private pay and other insurance.

That mix matters. A home where most residents are on Medicaid depends heavily on the Medicaid daily rate. Medicare usually pays more per day, but only for a limited time.

Where one resident day's money goes

To compare a 60-bed home with a 200-bed home, everything on this site is divided by resident days: one resident living in the home for one day. A home with 100 residents all year has about 36,500 resident days. "Per resident day" is what it costs to care for one person for one day.

How to read it

  • Nursing care is the biggest piece by far: nurses, nurse aides, agency staff and their benefits. DSS calls this direct care.
  • Dietary, housekeeping and laundry keep residents fed and their rooms and clothes clean. DSS groups these as indirect care.
  • Administrative and general covers the office, accounting, legal, and management fees paid to a management company or parent.
  • Building and property includes rent, depreciation and property taxes. Rent paid to a company owned by the same people (a related party) is common, and DSS treats it differently (see step 4).
Dietary, housekeeping and laundry on this site are all-in: wages + their share of benefits + supplies and contracts. Wages alone understate what a department costs.

    How Connecticut builds the Medicaid daily rate

    DSS sorts each home's allowed costs into five buckets, applies limits, and adds them up into one per diem (daily rate). Three buckets are capped at a percentage of what the typical (median) home spends, so a home that spends far more than its peers is not paid for all of it.

      Words to know

      Cost year

      October 1 to September 30. "Cost year 2025" means October 2024 through September 2025.

      Resident day

      One resident in the home for one day. Dividing by resident days lets you compare homes of different sizes.

      Direct care

      Nursing and nurse aide salaries, their fringe benefits, and nursing pool (agency) staff. This is how Connecticut law and DSS define it.

      Nursing pool / agency

      Nurses and aides hired through a staffing agency instead of as employees. Usually more expensive per hour.

      Related party

      A company connected to the home's owners, for example a management company or landlord owned by the same people. Homes must list these on the cost report. Being related is not wrongdoing; DSS limits what it counts.

      Fair rent

      A set yearly amount DSS pays for the building instead of the home's actual rent, mortgage interest or depreciation.

      Imputed days

      If a home is less than 90% full, DSS divides costs as if it were 90% full, so empty beds don't push the daily rate up.

      Acuity / case mix

      How much care residents need, measured from required federal assessments (MDS). DSS raises or lowers the direct care part of the rate each quarter based on it.

      Contractual allowance

      The difference between a home's standard charges and what a payer actually pays. "Medicaid revenue after contractual allowance" is what the home recorded as due from Medicaid.

      Operating margin

      (Revenue − expenses) ÷ revenue for the nursing home. Negative means the home reported spending more than it took in that year.

      What these numbers can't tell you

      • Whether care was good. Spending is not quality; see inspections and staffing for that.
      • Why DSS changed a line, or whether a later audit changed it again.
      • Exactly what Medicaid paid in claims. The site shows the home's recorded Medicaid revenue and DSS's published daily rates.
      • Whether a related-party price was fair. It shows only what was disclosed and what DSS counted.
